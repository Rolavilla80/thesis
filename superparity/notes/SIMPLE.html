Where should the transitions go? Results of RQ2

We found that parity removes a roughly constant fraction of the remaining fairness gap as its budget increases — at 80k vocabulary, every additional quarter of the merge budget given to parity halves the gap that remains. How much budget that takes depends mainly on vocabulary size; languages, data, and training samples enter only through the tokenizer's measured starting disparity E. Compression, meanwhile, barely depends on either switch — it is set by vocabulary size: within the safe regions the transitions cost under 1–2%, and a small parity ending actually improves compression, so training with no parity at all is strictly dominated.

That is the whole result. The rest of this page unpacks it into three answers, one table, and one procedure.

The question

A training run makes ~80,000 merge decisions and has two switches: t1 = when merges may start crossing spaces (BPE → SuperBPE), and t2 = when the objective changes from "most frequent overall" to "help the worst-compressed language" (classical → parity). Where should they go, and in what order? The answer came out in three different shapes.

One thing to notice first: this is really two questions in one. Where the safe regions are is a question of fact, with one answer. But the exact placement of t2 is a question of preference, because t2 is the compression–fairness dial, t1 tells when it is safe to become SuperBPE; t2 tells how much fairness you want to get. So t1 gets an answer, and t2 gets a menu plus a formula that turns a fairness target into a position.

Answer 1: t1 is a RANGE, but not really relevant

Anywhere in 10–50% of the budget (the total number of merges the run gets to make) is within <1% of the best compression, at every scale we measured (8k → 80k; the paper's own 200k curve agrees). Only the extremes hurt: before ~1–2k merges the multi-byte characters aren't assembled yet; very late starves the superword phase. t1 does nothing for fairness. It is not a sensitive hyperparameter: pick 0.2·V and move on.

Answer 2: ordering is a RULE (parity goes last)

Fairness has recency. In a budget-matched test at 80k (identical phase sizes, only the position of the parity block moved) placing parity in the middle and finishing classical erased the parity benefit completely: the final tokenizer was 99.95% merge-identical to one trained with no parity at all. Mechanism: early in a run, the two objectives pick nearly the same merges, and a classical finish re-derives whatever parity preempted; only at the end of a run does parity pick merges classical never would, and nothing comes after to undo them. Either way, it is better to finish on parity.

(This also settles the original BPE → parity → SuperBPE ordering: it stays a legitimate compression-first menu item (in the June runs it sat on the frontier's compressed end at every scale) but its middle parity block gets partially re-derived by the classical finish, so it cannot reach the fairness floor. Its strong June numbers came from its large parity share, one third of the budget, not from the ordering; a well-placed parity ending buys more fairness with a smaller share.)

Three rules of thumb, stable at every scale we tested (8k/20k/50k/80k):

  1. Never 0% parity. Giving parity the last ~10% improves compression and fairness at once; the no-parity endpoint is strictly dominated (confirmed at 4 scales, 3 language subsets, 3 data sizes, and an independent data draw).
  2. Last ~25% of the budget → disparity ≈ 1.20 at every vocabulary we measured (the "one half-life" point). A vocab-free landmark. (Two scale-trends cancel here: bigger vocabs start fairer but respond to parity more slowly; at 80k this point is exactly one half-life.)
  3. Last ~half → near the floor (~1.05); beyond half is nearly pure cost.

Using it on a new setting (the procedure)

Nothing extra needs to be trained to apply this:

  1. Train the classical spine (BPE → SuperBPE, t1 ≈ 0.2·V, no parity): it is needed anyway as phases 1–2 of the final tokenizer.
  2. Evaluate it → that disparity is E, the setting's starting unfairness. Languages, data size, and corpus draw all enter only through E.
  3. Choose a fairness target d, read off (or compute) the parity share b.
  4. Slice the spine at t2 = 1 − b (BPE is deterministic — a slice is a valid run) and train only the parity tail. The measurement costs nothing.

How much to trust it

~60 trained tokenizers across 4 vocab scales, 3 language subsets (3/6/10), and 3 data sizes; the full 21-cell (t1, t2) grid at 8k plus production-scale checks at 80k show the two switches don't interact (the sliced design loses nothing); key results reproduce out-of-domain (NTREX news) and on an independent training-data draw; all headline deltas carry bootstrap CIs. One notable bonus: the parity region of the menu is nearly invariant to the training draw — parity absorbs data-sampling variance; only the no-parity baseline E moves.

And the effect is visible in the tokens themselves, not just the curves: with a classical objective, ~62% of learned superwords are Latin-script; the parity phase redistributes them (Latin → ~29%, Cyrillic 13→38%, Devanagari 7→23%). Accordingly, the binding language changes: without parity the worst-treated language is Thai or Russian; at the fairness floor it becomes German, i.e. the weak scripts have been lifted until a high-resource European language is the constraint. That is what "parity" looks like up close.

Limits (volunteered up front)

~1 MB/language training data (curves may sharpen with more); intrinsic metrics only (no downstream LM); the vocab-scaling of the dial is fitted on 8k–80k — using it at 150k+ is an extrapolation with one falsifiable prediction on record (τ(150k) ≈ 0.39), testable when cluster/Rust compute is available.

Appendix, one line: the dial is disparity(b) = 1.02 + (E − 1.02)·e−b/τ, τ(V) = 0.037·log₂V − 0.25, with b = 1 − t2; fitted on 8k/20k/50k it predicts the held-out 80k frontier with RMSE 0.010. Details: rung2_synthesis.md §8, transition_findings.md.
